This repository holds my Sprint Review and Retrospective from CS 250. ## Reflection

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

For me it starts with listening to what the user is actually trying to get done, not just what they say they want built. Clients usually describe a feeling or a goal, like "I want customers to find trips that fit them," and it's on the team to turn that into something concrete we can build and test.

User stories are what make that translation work. Writing each need as "As a [user], I want [something] so that [reason]" forces the team to keep the person and the purpose in front of the feature. Adding acceptance criteria to each story gives developers and testers the same definition of done, so nobody is guessing. On SNHU Travel, the stories about vacation preferences turned straight into test cases, and when we found that a list of destinations wasn't what users wanted, we revised the story and the test case into a slideshow. Because the need was written down clearly, changing direction was a small adjustment instead of a rebuild.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I approach development in small pieces that I can actually check. Instead of planning everything up front and hoping it holds, I'd rather build something small, get feedback on it, and adjust. This course showed me why that matters. When the client pivoted toward wellness and detox trips, we kept the parts of the slideshow that already worked and swapped out the content, which only worked because we hadn't locked ourselves into one giant plan.

The Agile practices I plan to keep using:

- **A prioritized backlog** so there's always one place that shows what matters most right now.
- **Short sprints with a real Sprint Review** so feedback comes in while it's still cheap to act on.
- **Daily stand-ups** to surface blockers fast instead of letting them sit for a week.
- **Retrospectives** that end with one or two specific changes, not a wish list nobody follows through on.
- **Writing tests alongside development** instead of saving testing for the end.

I already lean on a lot of this in my day job running bar operations, where I build tools in short iterations and adjust them based on how the team actually uses them. This course gave me the vocabulary and structure behind what I'd been doing by instinct.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member means making the people around you more effective, not just finishing your own tickets. A few things stood out to me:

- **Communicate early and clearly.** When requirements changed, the most useful messages were the ones that told the tester exactly what changed, what didn't, and what I needed from them. That saved a meeting and kept work moving.
- **Respect the roles.** The Product Owner owns priorities, the Scrum Master protects the process, and developers and testers own the work. Things run smoother when everyone trusts each other to do their part.
- **Keep shared tools honest.** A task board is only useful if people keep it updated. A stale board is worse than no board, because people trust it.
- **Be honest in retrospectives.** The team only improves if people are willing to say what slowed them down, including their own mistakes.
- **Stay flexible.** Priorities shift. A good teammate adjusts without getting defensive and helps the team figure out what still fits the deadline.

At the end of the day, good software comes from a team that talks to each other, trusts each other, and keeps the user at the center of every decision.
